GLADSTONE TOWN CENTER BOUNDARY AND IMPLEMENTATION MEASURES 1
 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

T
he Gladstone Town Center boundary honors the work done through adoption of the 2017 Downtown
Revitalization Plan and 2023 Downtown Core Overlay District. The chosen boundary focuses on
opportunities for economic development and housing options while expanding flexibility for transportation
choices.

T
he boundary incorporates the Portland Avenue corridor, connecting all commercially zoned parcels along
the city’s designated Main Street to the Clackamas River at the south, and expanding east and west to
include a small share of the city’s existing residential lands, as well as the economic development
opportunities along 82nd Drive and transit connectivity along McLoughlin Boulevard.

T
he city of Gladstone envisions a Town Center that creates a focal point of community life and commerce,
providing opportunities for residents and business owners to connect, eat, shop, work, and play. Getting to
and around the Town Center should be safe and easy, whether traveling by foot, bike, skateboard, or on a
bus or in a car. A greater mix of housing, restaurants, shops, parks and public gathering spaces, fronting
tree-lined streets, will support existing businesses and attract new businesses and visitors.

T
he designation of Gladstone’s Town Center boundary considers existing development, market factors,
displacement risks and anti-displacement strategies within the chosen boundary. The boundary will help
direct new development regulations, policies, and market investments to support Gladstone’s overall
livability and economic vitality and better serve current and future Gladstone community members.

BACKGROUND
Gladstone is a historic city with a proud past and a
bright future. Approximately 12,000 residents call the
city home. Gladstone is home to many amenities for
outdoor enthusiasts, including parks, trails, ball fields,
community gardens and access to both the
Willamette and Clackamas rivers, with a boat ramp
for enthusiasts. Community events throughout the
year ensure residents and visitors have ample
opportunity to gather and celebrate. The Portland
Avenue corridor is the historic downtown, home to
many locally owned and operated businesses.
I
n 2017, the City of Gladstone adopted the Downtown
Revitalization Plan, a future vision for Portland Avenue
with renewed business activity, local investment and
connectivity for pedestrians, bikes and vehicles.
I
n 2023, the City continued the vision of the
Downtown Revitalization Plan by adopting a new
Downtown Core Overlay District, applying
development code standards that promote transit-
supportive and mixed-use development, encourage
the development and redevelopment of vacant or
underused parcels and support a mix of
transportation options.
I
n 2024, the City expanded the Urban Renewal
District to include all properties in the C-2 zoning
district along Portland Avenue, increasing the City’s
ability to leverage public investment to spur economic
development.
Al
so in 2024, the City applied for and received a State
of Oregon Department of Land Conservation and
Development (DLCD) Climate-Friendly and Equitable
Communities (CFEC) Grant to fund parking reform
throughout the city (completed in late 2024) and to
fund the Gladstone Town Center project.
T
he Gladstone Town Center will be a climate Friendly
Area (CFA) in accordance with the CFEC rulemaking
(OAR 660-012-0310), which requires cities in metro
areas to take urgent action to meet Oregon’s climate
pollution reduction targets. The rules encourage
climate-friendly development by facilitating areas
where residents, workers, and visitors can meet most
of their daily needs without having to drive. The CFA
aims to contain a variety of housing, jobs, businesses,
and services. A CFA also supports alternative modes
of transportation by being in close proximity to high-
quality pedestrian, bicycle, and transit infrastructure.

 PROCESS
Throughout 2024, the City undertook the task of
convening a Stakeholder Advisory Committee (SAC) to
review materials, provide feedback and guide the
selection of the Gladstone Town Center Boundary and
implementation measures. The SAC met three times
throughout the year.
T
he process kicked off with the first SAC meeting
where participants were provided with a project
overview and background information and were
asked to reflect on what they liked about Gladstone,
what made the city special, what the city could do
better, and where the city should focus the Town
Center project.
A
t the second SAC meeting, participants were
provided with three draft Town Center boundary
options, as well as an evaluation of the market
factors and displacement risks and strategies for
each boundary. The boundaries all focused on the
Portland Avenue commercial corridor and varying
amounts of additional residential and commercial
land. Participants reviewed and discussed, ultimately
choosing two boundaries to provide for public review
and comment at a Community meeting.
In
 August of 2024, the project team held a
Community Meeting to provide information and
solicit input about the Town Center boundary. The
public meeting was attended by 14 community
members who reviewed materials, asked questions,
discussed the Town Center and provided their input
regarding the pros and cons of each boundary
option, with some even suggesting a third boundary
option for consideration.

Even the best Town Center Plan effort
won’t gain much traction without actual
public dollars to be strategically
deployed as a catalyst to private
investment in the community. Urban
renewal is one of the most powerful
funding tools for local governments in
the state of Oregon to plan, finance,
and build public and private projects.

At the third and final SAC meeting, participants were
provided with feedback from the community meeting
as well as from a joint Planning Commission/City

participants did, however, provide their feedback on
opportunities and concerns for each boundary option
and potential implementation measures.

PREFERRED BOUNDARY
The feedback from the final SAC meeting was
provided to decision makers at a joint City
Council/Planning Commission work session in
September 2024. The boundary options reviewed
by the SAC were provided, along with a staff
recommendation based on the goals of the
project and community feedback. At this work
session, staff recommended, and decision makers
generally supported, the boundary option shaped
like an inverted T, incorporating:
• T

zoned properties) along Portland Avenue.
• A
 stretch of the Clackamas River frontage.

• G
eneral Commercial (C-3 zoned

MPLEMENTATION MEASURES
The Planning Commission and City Council were
interested in modest implementation measures to
support the Town Center Boundary, particularly
focused on economic development and expanded
housing opportunities. To build upon the adopted
Downtown Core Overlay District (applicable in the C-2
zoning in the 4-block stretch along Portland Avenue
from Arlington Street to Exeter Street), the expanded
Urban Renewal District and changes to housing
development codes in recent years, the Gladstone
Town Center Plan includes the following
implementation measures:
• E
xpanding the Downtown Core Overlay District to
include all properties zoned C-2 (Community
Commercial) within the Town Center boundary.
• A
llowing mixed-use development outright on all
properties zoned C-3 (General Commercial) and R-
5 within the Town Center boundary.
• R
educing the minimum lot size for single-
household and multi-household dwellings and
reducing the front and street side setbacks for
properties zoned R-5 (Single-Household.
Residential) within the Town Center boundary
• A
dding policies to the Economy and Housing
chapters of the Gladstone Comprehensive Plan to
support a vibrant Gladstone Town Center and
mitigate displacement within the Town Center
boundary.

DISPLACEMENT RISK AND
MITIGATION STRATEGIES
Given the regulatory changes required for
implementing the Town Center Boundary, an
assessment of displacement risk and potential
mitigation measures was conducted as part of this
process. The primary risk of displacement affects
small rental housing properties; homeowners are less
impacted since they have more control over property
decisions. Key displacement risks associated with
different zoning changes include:
• Community Commercial (C2) Zone: Expansion of
the Downtown Core Overlay district could lead
to the redevelopment of 5 existing multiplex
properties into commercial or higher-density
mixed-use developments. This poses a marginal
increase in displacement risk.

• General Commercial (C3) Zone: Allowing mixed-
use development outright could increase
redevelopment potential for 5 single-detached
rental homes at the east end of Arlington
Street. However, the cost and difficulty of lot
aggregation might reduce redevelopment
likelihood.
• Single-Family Residential (R5) Zone: Changes
include allowing 5-8 unit housing outright and
reducing lot size requirements, making
redevelopment of smaller rental properties
more feasible. About 7 multiplex properties in
this zone could be redeveloped, leading to a
moderate increase in displacement risk.
W
hile the total number of affected households is
relatively small, these zoning changes could increase
the risk of displacement for renters living in lower-
cost housing.
T
hrough Comprehensive Plan policy amendments,
the Town Center Plan implements several measures
to support tenants and balance redevelopment goals
with housing stability, such as:
• Tenant Protections: Partner with Clackamas
County and non-profits to provide legal aid,
eviction prevention assistance, and emergency
rental support.
• Weatherization Programs: Support home
improvement programs for low-income
homeowners and small rental property owners
to maintain affordability.
• Incentives for Property Owners: Partner with
Clackamas County Housing Authority to provide
incentives for existing property owners such as
tax abatements, grants, and/or loans to make
needed health and safety improvements.
• Increase Housing Supply: Encourage new
housing developments to alleviate market
pressures and prevent sharp rent increases.
W
hile redevelopment under the new zoning
regulations could lead to displacement risks for some
renters in the Town Center area, targeted mitigation
strategies can help balance economic growth with
housing stability. The City’s role in collaboration with
county and non-profit organizations will be crucial in
minimizing negative impacts while fostering a more
vibrant and inclusive Town Center.
CONCLUSION
The Gladstone Town Center Plan represents a
significant step toward fostering economic growth,
increasing housing opportunities, and enhancing
community livability while aligning with climate-
friendly urban development principles. The Town
Center Boundary integrates commercial, residential,
and transit corridors to create a vibrant and accessible
urban core.
K
ey implementation measures include expanding
mixed-use zoning, reducing lot size requirements for
housing, and updating economic and housing policies
to support a more dynamic and inclusive Town
Center. These regulatory changes aim to increase
redevelopment potential, attract investment, and
improve connectivity while preserving the city's
character.
Wh
ile the potential for displacement risk is small, new
policies around tenant protections, property owner
incentives, and reducing barriers to increasing the
supply of housing ensures that growth benefits all
community members.
M
oving forward, collaboration between the City,
Clackamas County, Metro, DLCD and local
organizations will be essential to supporting
Gladstone’s overall livability and economic vitality and
better serve current and future Gladstone community
members.
