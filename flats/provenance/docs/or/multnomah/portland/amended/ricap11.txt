Section I: Staff Report

I.       Project Summary

The Regulatory Improvement Code Amendment Package 11 (RICAP 11) of amendments is the most
recent installment    in an ongoing series of updates and improvements to Portland’s land use
regulations. The purpose of this series is to        clarify regulations and remove barriers to development
across the city. This document contains the proposed code amendments and commentary for the
more than 50 items. The amendments have been bundled into three themes: parking, exterior
areas, and general regulatory improvement. The project also contains seven amendments to Title
32, Signs and Related Regulations         to address signs in City parks      .

II.      Background

Portland’s current Zoning Code was adopted in 1990. Over time, changing needs, new laws and court
rulings, new technology and innovations, and shifting per             spectives have necessitated updates and
improvements to regulations. To help stay current with              these changes, t    he Regulatory Improvement
program began in 2002 to “update and improve City building and land use regulations that hinder
desirable development.” One component of the program was RICAP, which was designed to provide
an ongoing vehicle for technical and min          or policy amendments to the City’s regulations. From 2003
to 2010, the City Council adopted eight packages of amendments (Policy Packages 1                       -3 and RICAPs 1
through 5), which resulted in many amendments to city regulations. Most of the changes were to
zoning code regulations. Following a suspension of the program due to budget limitations from
2010 to 2013, the program was reinstated in 2014 and RICAP                 s 6 through 8 followed. Another
suspension of the program occurred from 2016 to 2022, also due to budget constraints.

In 2022, the City’s Permitting Improvement Task Force highlighted the need for continuous
improvement of the City’s zoning regulations and identified the value the RICAP program had
provided while it was funded. As a result, the program was reinstated with                  the RICAP 10 project.
RICAP 11 is the next installment of the Regulatory Improvement program.

Workplan Selection Background
Generally, requests for both process and regulatory improvements are submitted by members of the
public and City staff through       an online portal    called the Regulatory Improvement Request (             RIR)
database. Staff with the Bureau of Planning and Sustainability (BPS) and Portland                   Permitting   &
Development (PP&D) categorize the requests according to complexity and resources needed to
address the issue. Items related to issues that could result in more significant policy changes, or
would require significant resources, are directed to other legi            slative projects. The remaining issues
are considered for inclusion into RICAP.

The items are further categorized as “minor policy” or “technical amendments” based on complexity
and level of impact:

August 2026                                 RICAP 11—As-Adopted                                                 Page 7
    1)   Minor policy items are those that may affect existing policy, for example by expanding or
         restricting allowed uses, changing the review type or procedure for land use applications, or
         revising development standards.

    2)   Technical corrections, clarifications, or consistency items are those where a particular
         requirement is unclear, or the regulations conflict with other sections of the code.

Minor policy items are ranked using the following criteria:

     a.   The variety of stakeholders an issue affects (Few people or many? One                    community or
          several?);
     b.   The geographic applicability of an issue (Is it a citywide regulation or one that affects a
          particular area?);
     c.   The degree of impact (in terms of severity or frequency) that an issue may have; and
     d.   A “regulatory improvement” component, which is an estimate of the degree that the
          regulation can be improved due to its current complexity or rigidity.

Each of the four criteria are ranked between (-3) and (+3), so that the sum of the four criteria range
between (-12) and (+12). An item that ranks as a zero would fall in the middle range for these
criteria.

To develop the RICAP workplan, staff consider the complexity, rank, and resources needed to
address the issue. Generally, the most important items that fit within the scope of a RICAP rise to the
top of the ranking process. Not all top-ranked items are selected. Selection is also based on
resources, the relationship of the item to other pending city projects, and the need to consider the
item as part of a more holistic planning process.
The RICAP 11 Workplan
In recent years there has been an emphasis on making city regulations less restrictive and processes
more efficient. This objective drives the RICAP 11 work plan as well, with its emphasis on easing
regulatory burdens and making zoning and sign codes easier to understand and implement.  While
the impacts of individual RICAP amendments are intentionally small, taken together the amendments
contribute to this objective.

First, the RICAP project team, comprised of staff from BPS and PP&D, sorted through the more than
400 eligible technical and minor policy workplan items using the ranking methodology discussed
above. The team then selected amendments for this package from the higher ranked items based on
themes and groupings that emerged and based on timeliness and relevance to the current moment.
Two main themes emerged: items related to parking and loading as well as items related to
exterior development and uses. A third theme of general regulatory improvement is a collection
of items that fit into smaller groupings or individually contribute to regulatory improvement.

In past RICAPs, smaller technical items were generally added to the workplan automatically; however,
the suspension of the program over several years resulted in a surfeit of items. For RICAP 11,
technical items were added if they fit within one of the themes or were identified as being of higher
priority.

Finally, in addition to items that originated from RIR database entries, project staff often receive
requests for items that address more pressing issues. These may bypass the database ranking
process and get added directly to the workplan during the early stages of the project, as several were
with RICAP 11.

The resulting RICAP 11 work plan contains over 50 items.

III. RICAP 11 Amendments and Themes

Below is a description of each of the RICAP 11 themes. The table of amendments on the following
pages lists the items by topic and bundle.          Later in this report, in the code and commentary section,
the code amendments are in the zoning code chapter/section order.                    As noted above, this project
also contains seven amendments to Title 32, Signs and Related Regulations, which are                       found in
Section VI of this report.
Parking and Loading
These 10 amendments focus on items that              clarify, rework, or address loopholes        related to the
requirements for vehicle and        bicycle parking.    Two of the larger topics addressed are parking in
residential setbacks and whether a change of use triggers               bike parking    upgrades.
Exterior Areas
The 16 exterior area amendments modify standards that regulate exterior improvements on a site
that are not inside of a building. The topics address issues including what is allowed in the setbacks
or outdoor areas, how development must be screened or landscaped, and how exterior areas are
measured for the purpose of determining the extent of where limited uses can locate.
Regulatory Improvement
These 31 amendments cover an array of topics with a focus on eliminating obsolete and unnecessary
regulations and clarifying rules to make the permitting process easier.                The topics covered      range from
outdoor shelter allowances to         use category clarifications.

IV.    Community Engagement
The following outlines the outreach efforts aimed at informing and engaging community members in
regard to RICAP 11.
Project Scoping
The first opportunity the community has to get involved with the RICAP process is to submit code
update requests via the Regulatory Improvement Request (RIR) database. Developing the project
scope for RICAP 11 began with reviewing, sorting, and ranking the over 400 RIR items, many of which
have been submitted by members of the public, and then identifying themes that emerge from the
ranking process. Several of the items included in RICAP 11 were either submit                  ted directly by
community members or are a response to a need that project staff was made aware of, usually
through the review of development proposals             . Once the issues to be resolved were          compiled into
the RICAP 11 work plan, it was posted on the project website                and announced in       BPS’s Long-Range
Planning newsletter and BPS’s eNews. It was also shared with the Development Review Advisory
Committee.
Project Website
The RICAP 11 website (portland.gov/bps/planning/ricap11) provides summary information on the
project, presents project updates and information on how to provide feedback, and allows for
interested community members to sign up for project notifications. The website also includes links to
project documents and resources, as well as to the Map App, where the community can submit
comments and view documents.
Discussion Draft
The RICAP 11 Discussion Draft was published on June 13, 2025. The Discussion Draft is the first
opportunity to share the RICAP 11 proposals with the broader public and to seek feedback to inform
refinements to the project proposals. Outreach for the Discussion Draft include                  d:
  •    Publicizing the Discussion Draft on the project website and through BPS news updates.
  •    Launching an online Map App to provide an opportunity for community members to submit
       comments.
  •    Offering to present the proposal to district coalition committees, neighborhood associations,
       and other community interests.

TA18 – Daycare uses in buildings with previous daycare use
33.110.100
Daycare uses are allowed by right in a building that contains or contained a College,
Medical Center, School, Religious Institution or a Community Service use. Buildings that
contain or contained a Daycare use are not included in the list, so buildings that
historically housed a daycare but none of the other listed uses are required to go through
a CU review to be approved. This runs counter to the State of Oregon initiative to expand
opportunities for daycare operations to address the statewide daycare shortage.
Therefore, the zoning code is being updated to allow Daycare use outright in a building
that contains or previously contained a Daycare use.

space is floor area.

TA10 – Screening for mechanical equipment
33.110.245.F.2.b
Under the existing code, mechanical equipment in residential zones may be located in a setback
provided that it is not taller than 5 feet high and is screened from adjoining lots. There has
been concern that the screening requirement discourages the installation of heat pumps and
other equipment that support decarbonization and electrification goals. There have also been
concerns that the required screening interferes with the operation of such equipment by
blocking intake air flow. To address these concerns, this amendment eliminates the
requirement to screen mechanical equipment from adjoining lots, provided that the equipment
is no taller, longer, or wider than 5 feet.

MP11 – Institutional buffering standards
33.110.270.C.8
When a landscape buffer is required for an institutional use, there is no code guidance on
whether interruptions are allowed for things such as a pedestrian path. The only allowance is
for grassy play areas, golf courses, cemeteries, and natural areas, which do not have to comply
with the landscape buffer standard at all. This has led to adjustment reviews for any other
type of intrusion into a required buffer.

There are other, similar instances in the code where a landscape buffer is required and
incursions or interruptions are more clearly laid out. For example, Section 33.130.215.B
contains the minimum setback requirements in the commercial zones adjacent to residential
zones. The setback must be landscaped, but there are exceptions                  that allow incursions, such as
for bicycle and pedestrian areas and minor building elements, as long as they do not come
closer than 5 feet to the property line abutting the residential zone. There is also an exception
that allows a driveway to cross the landscape buffer provided that all of the street frontages
on the site abut a residential zone. The Buffer overlay zone similarly has requirements for
buffers adjacent to residential areas and includes an exception for pedestrian bicycle paths
that cut through the landscape buffer to provide access to the site.

The amendments to this section recognize the need for some incursions into the landscape
buffer and borrow from some of the concepts in the abovementioned chapters that have
worked in other zones for non-residential development adjacent to residential areas.

TA19 – Daycare uses in multi-dwelling zones
33.120.100
House Bill 3560 (HB 3560) was passed by the Oregon legislature during the 2025 regular
session. The bill amended ORS 329A.440, which addresses the application of land use
regulations to childcare, and requires jurisdictions to expand where childcare centers are
allowed without triggering a land use review. Portland’s “Daycare” use category aligns with the
state’s definition of a “childcare center.” Note that the bill also directed the language about
siting child care centers in ORS 329A.440 move to ORS 197. It is now found in ORS 197.671

Section 2 of HB3560 amends ORS 329A.440 to require municipalities to allow childcare
centers as a permitted use in 3 situations, two of which are new:
    1)   On land zoned primarily for commercial and industrial use, except in areas specifically
         designated for heavy industrial uses. (This was previously in ORS.) Portland allows
         childcare centers (Daycare) in commercial and employment zones, with limits in
         industrial zones that are designated for heavy industrial use.
    2)   On sites with a legally permitted institutional use in the residential zones. Portland’s
         zoning code already allows daycare in conjunction with an institutional use in both
         single-dwelling and multi-dwelling zones.
    3)   In areas “zoned primarily for multiunit residential uses and” at a density of 17 dwelling
         units per net acre within the Metro urban growth boundary. This equates to Portland’s
         multi-dwelling zones RM1-RM4. (The RX zone allowed Daycare uses by right.) Portland’s
         zoning code allows daycare uses in the multi-dwelling zones, but includes limits on either
         the size or the location of the childcare. A daycare that cannot meet those provisions
         triggers a conditional use review.

This amendment removes the current limitations on Daycare uses within the multi-dwelling
zones, which aligns with the intent of the provisions in HB 3560. With the change,                    Daycare
uses are allowed within these zones by-right, without any limitations or conditional use review
triggers.

MP11 – Institutional buffering standards
33.120.275
For a required landscape buffer for in         stitutional uses, there is no code guidance on when
interruptions are allowed for such things as a pedestrian path. The only allowance is for grassy
play areas, golf courses, cemeteries, and natural areas, which do not have to comply with the
landscape buffer standard. This has led to adjustment reviews for any other type of intrusion
into a required buffer.

There are other, similar instances in the code where a landscape buffer is                  required, and
allowed incursions and interruptions are more clearly laid out. Section 33.130.215.B contains
the minimum setback requirements           for sites in a commercial/mixed-use zone adjacent to a
residential zone. The setback must be landscaped, but there are a number of exceptions to the
landscaped setback requirement, such as for bicycle and pedestrian areas and minor building
elements; however, the incursion can come no closer than 5 feet to the property line abutting a
residential zone. There also is an exception to allow a driveway to cross the landscape buffer
provided that all of the sites street frontages adjoin residential zones. The Buffer overlay
zone similarly has requirements that buffer residential areas from non-residential uses and
those regulations include an exception for pedestrian bicycle paths that cut through the
landscape buffer to allow access to the site.

The amendments to this section recognize the need for some incursions into the landscape
buffer and borrow from some of the concepts in the abovementioned chapters that have
worked in other zones for non-residential development adjacent to residential areas.

MP3 – Parking in residential setbacks
33.266.120.C
Currently, the zoning code does not allow parking within 10 feet of the front lot line for
houses, duplexes, triplexes and fourplexes. This limitation can be a barrier to converting a
garage to living space, such as an ADU, when a property owner wants to c                  ontinue to park in

to not incent the creation of a new parking space in the front setback on a site that didn’t

MP13 – Outdoor shelter allowances
33.285.040.C.2 and C.3
The City of Portland has declared         a housing state of emergency        , which was first declared in
2015. The emergency declaration, which was recently extended                   to October 25, 2027, allows
outdoor and other types of shelters to operate out of compliance with the zoning code
regulations for the duration of the emergency.

The Shelter to Housing Continuum p          rojects (S2HC and S2HC2), adopted in 2020 and 2022                  ,
adopted new standards        for outdoor shelters      with the intent of making it easier to site          this new
type of shelters while also setting        parameters to limit impacts to        surrounding neighbors. The
standards currently allow a maximum of 30 to 60 individual outdoor shelter units, depending on
zoning, beyond which a conditional use review is required.

Because of the housing emergency declaration, these standards have never been applied and
many of the City’s Safe Rest Village outdoor shelter            s have more than 60 individual shelter
units (up to 160 units in some locations). In the face of the ongoing houselessness crisis in
Portland, it is imperative that all of the existing outdoor shelters are allowed to maintain
operations at the current levels when the declared housing emergency ends in 2027. To that
end, this amendment raises the thresholds to 60, 100, and 200 individual outdoor shelter units,
depending on zoning, before a conditional use review is triggered. The proposed thresholds
account for the capacity of existing outdoor shelters with a small buffer to provide for
additional flexibility

While outdoor shelters are intended to provide temporary housing, the unit density is
comparable to other types of housing. For example, the scale of housing allowed in zones where
the maximum number of outdoor shelter units allowed without conditional use review is being
increased to 100 or 200 units (RM3-RX, C zones, EX, CI2), in most cases allows a base height
of 45-75 feet, base FAR of 2.5:1 or greater, and at least 85% building coverage. This would
allow a multi-dwelling or mixed-use development with far more dwelling units and residents
than would be allowed in an outdoor shelter that meets standards. The off-site impacts of a
large multi-dwelling or mixed-use development (e.g. noise, activity, light) is comparable to an
outdoor shelter. Furthermore, the City’s outdoor shelters all have either a good neighbor
agreement (GNA)       or a good neighbor commitment (GNC)             in place. These agreements        address
the types of issues    /impacts that are addressed          as part of a   conditional use    review, including
physical compatibility of the shelter with the surrounding neighborhood                  and livability issues
related to off   -site impacts and public safety       , requirements to remove unsanctioned camping, as
well as staffing and communication conditions           .

MP13 – Outdoor shelter allowances
33.285.040.C.4 and 33.285.050.C.2
The City of Portland has declared         a housing state of emergency         , which  was first declared in
2015. The emergency declaration, which was recently extended                   to October 25, 2027, allows
outdoor and other types of shelters to operate out of compliance with the zoning code
regulations for the duration of the emergency.

The Shelter to Housing Continuum projects, adopted in 2020 and 2022, created the outdoor
shelter type and put in place a requirement for a conditional use review to establish an outdoor
shelter in a general employment (EG) or industrial (I) zone. Since 2020, several outdoor
shelters have been established in         EG or I zones, and because of the declared housing
emergency, no conditional use review          has been required. When the state of emergency is
lifted, these outdoor shelters        will no longer be able to operate without a         n approved conditional
use review. In addition, at least one of the shelters is over the maximum size limit of 2 acres                     in
industrial zones.

In the interest of continuing to provide shelter for people experiencing homelessness, this
amendment allows the existing outdoor shelters in              EG and I zones to continue without a
conditional use review after the effective date of RICAP 11 (anticipated to be                    in August of
2026). After the emergency is lifted, any new outdoor shelters in an I or EG zone will require a
conditional use. Outdoor shelters in I zones will also be required to meet the size limitation of
2 acres.

Staff considered the effects of allowing these shelters to continue to operate without going
through the conditional use review and found that all of the shelters have a good neighbor
agreement (GNA) that addresses the same types of issues as would be addressed as part of
conditional use review. Conditional use review approval criteria address the physical
compatibility of the shelter with the surrounding neighborhood, potential livability issues
related to off-site impacts and public safety, as well as public services, mostly having to do
with the transportation system’s ability to handle any additional capacity needs. Issues of
physical compatibility are low, since the development footprint of an outdoor shelter is limited
and there are robust screening and setback requirements in place. Transportation impacts are
also limited because very few residents of most outdoor shelter own cars, and with regard to
the Safe Park Villages, the intent is for the vehicles to be able to park for extended periods
rather than coming and going on City streets. Finally, the livability impacts are equally or
better handled through a good neighbor agreement because issues related to off-site impacts
and public safety are addressed through agreements related to quiet hours, requirements to
remove unsanctioned camping, as well as staffing and communication conditions.

County Recorder before the approval           can be implemented. In the past, it was the responsibility
of the applicant to record the decision before a building permit could be issued or other action
could be taken. More recently, PP&D staff have taken on the responsibility for recording the
approval decisions to avoid permitting delays that resulted from applicants not recording
decisions. However, recording land use decisions is costly for the City, and in light of the fact
that land use case files and final decisions can now be found in the City’s online archives
system, it may be an unwarranted cost.

This amendment removes the recording requirement for many land use review types to reduce
the cost and burden on PP&D staff. The list of land use review decisions for which recording
with the County recorder will continue to be required is narrowed to those that are most likely
to include conditions of approval that affect the use of a site. Land use reviews for Conditional
Uses, Planned Developments, Comprehensive Plan Amendments, and Zoning Map Amendments
will continue to be recorded so that future buyers and title companies will see any conditions
of approval on a title report. This amendment does not affect               the recording requirements         for
other key documents, such as covenants or easements, that may be required as a condition of a
land use review.

Background
The following regulations add flexibility to the sign code for Parks And Open Areas uses,
with a focus on larger parks. The intent is to allow a modest amount of signage at public
parks and to treat parks and open areas similar to other community serving areas and
school sites. Large park areas often require signage to help orient visitors to the area. The
changes also provide an opportunity for parks, schools, and other institutional and
community serving uses to provide           small changing image signs that can provide up to date
messaging such as special events, while acknowledging that regionally serving parks such as
Washington Park may need additional flexibility.

zoning code (Title 33), due to changes made in Title 33 over time. The definition                     of right-

titles. However, over time the right-of-way definition in the zoning code has been amended
without corresponding amendments in the sign code to keep them aligned.

This amendment updates the definition of right              -of-way in the sign code to match the
definition that is in the zoning code. This clarifies the original intent that the sign code’s land
use regulations are not intended to apply to signage that is along vehic                le or pedestrian
accessways internal to the site not formally recognized as public or private rights-of-way, as
long as the signage is also not visible from adjoining properties.

This table provides information regulating specific features of signs including changing image
signs and signs that project into the right-of-way. However, the main distinction within the

and activities for visitors and students and can benefit from having small changing image signs
that include moving information or reader boards for those visiting the site for special parks
events and school events such as graduations or games. In the case of Washington park, a
changing image sign can aid visitors to find available parking spaces, eliminating the need                   for
visitors to circle the parking lots in search of an available spot.

The amendment moves the RX zone from the column under which changing image signs are
prohibited to the column that allows modest changing image signs. The provision still                    directs
readers to the special regulations that apply to changing image signs, which limit their overall
size and do not allow adjustments except in specific situations.
