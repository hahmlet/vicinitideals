Section I: Introduction
Why this project is necessary
The State Land Use Compliance P  roject responds to several state bills that were passed in the
2025 and 2026 State of Oregon                                        legislative sessions that affect Portland’s zoning code and land
use review procedures. Some of these               bills are already in effect, while others will              become
effective at various future dates.
Applying the bills directly to development applications and building permits     without amending
local land use regulations is an option for cities. For some aspects of House Bill 2138, the City
would be required to apply the State’s model code, unless the City’s zoning code is updated.
The State Land Use Compliance Project proposes   to address the bills by amending Portland’s
local ordinances. This avoids development review applicants and             review staff needing to
interpret language from the legislation and translate it to various local zoning requirements.
This can be challenging as state statutes              use different term inology or regulatory structures than
the City. The result is                              ambiguous and inconsistent application of regulations causing uncertainty
and frustration.   Navigating this web of bills, local code, and model code threatens to slow
reviews, increase the chance for errors, and generates co            nfusion for applicants               and the public .
The State Land Use Compliance project ensure                                 s Portland remains compliant with state law, and
helps the development community   and the public   understand                             how the state            rule changes work
with other aspects of  the City code.
Summary of Relevant State Land Use Bills
This introduction summarizes                   the portions of the bills that are being addressed by this project.
The full text of the bills can be found in the appendi                 ces and on the  State of Oregon Legislative
Information Service webpage: www.oregonlegislature.gov/bills_laws/pages/ors.aspx.
Section II of this report, Proposals, explains the  proposed zoning code changes to implement
these bills. The code and associated commentary             can be found in Section IV                                                      ,              Zoning Code
Amendments.

Effective dates:
      July 17, 2025                                                                           Changes to the   middle housing land division process.

      Jan. 1, 2027                                                                                                                            Other provisions, primarily related to middle housing development

Middle housing is a term used to describe housing forms that are compatible in scale with
single dwelling houses but that                   accommodate more units. The statutory definition includes
development such as duplexes, triplexes, fourplexes and cottage clusters                          . House Bill 2138
amends the definitions of middle housing types, expands the geographies                   where middle
housing is required to be allowed, requires cities to offer density  bonuses to encourage middle
housing, and revises the middle housing land division and expedited land division procedures.
This project will address these portions of the bill.
HB 2138 also                                                                                                                                   includes other statutory changes that are not addressed by this project                , such as
those that apply to single room occupancy housing, Covenants, Conditions, and Restrictions
(CC&Rs), and directives to Department of Land Conservation and Development (DLCD) for
additional State rulemaking.
Senate Bill 974 - Limiting Hearings on Certain Land Use Applications  –
Effective September 26, 2025
Senate Bill 974 affects the land use   review procedures for three land use reviews:                               zone changes
that increase residential density, planned developments, and adjustments to a residential
development standard. The bill overrides local procedures and  specifies that notice for these
applications be   provided to property owners no further than               100 feet of a site and requires that
the initial decision be made without a public                hearing. This bill does not override local
application review timelines and allows, but does not require , cities to retain a local appeal
opportunity. This project will address these portions of the bill.
SB 974 also includes provisions that are not addressed by this project because they                                                do not
affect the zoning code or are inapplicable within the context of the city’s zoning code. Those
changes include different               timelines for engineering plan review and limitations on certain
residential design standards for projects with more than 20 housing units but that are not in a
multifamily building (as defined in state statute as 3 or more units).
HB 4037 –             Limiting Notice for Clear and Objective Reviews   -
Effective June 5, 2026
There are two provisions in House Bill 4037 that directly affect the City’s zoning code. The first
applies to residential development applications that are reviewed using clear and objective (i.e.
non-discretionary) standards. These include building and zoning permits that do not require a
land use review             and environmental zoning plan checks   . It could potentially include some land
divisions where the application meets the clear and objective land division standards and does
not use, or need to use, the alternative discretionary approval criteria. HB 4037 modifies the

October 2026                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                      State Land Use Compliance Project            — As Adopted                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                        Page 9
clear and objective review statutes by specifying the amount of notice that may be provided,
restricts public hearings, and limits appeals to the applicant only.
The second is a technical fix to align the middle housing land division and regular land division
plat expiration timelines when the two types of review are consolidated.       This project will
address both provisions.
HB 4037 is an omnibus bill. It includes  non-land use changes              that are not addressed by this
project. The bill includes provisions related to c             ity and county housing project funding programs
for low and moderate income households; modifies certain grant terms for community
development financial institutions  ; expands the Land Conservation and Development
Commission’s (LCDC) authority to issue housing statute enforcement orders to small cities;
directs LCDC to amend its urban reserve rules by January 2027; allows expedited building plan
review for house and duplex plans when prepared by a registered engineer; clarifies
tenant/landlord requirements when homes are destroyed by a natural disaster; expands use of
state lands for housing for moderate income households; and provides for some commercial
use portions of large planned developments to convert to residential use for affordable
housing. These changes do not affect zoning and are not addressed by this project.
House Bill 2005 – Residential Treatment Facilities –
Effective July 24, 2025
House Bill 2005 mandates that local governments allow the siting of residential treatment
facilities, residential treatment homes, and crisis stabilization centers by right within urban
growth boundaries  on land zoned for residential ,             commercial, or employment uses.                     The bill
streamlines zoning by   narrowing the ability of cities to limit               these facilities through land                                                           use
regulations, for example by requiring a                                      zone change or conditional use review                                           to site a
facility. This project will address these portions of the bill .
HB 2005                                                                                                             also includes some non-                             land use changes  that are not addressed by this project.    These
changes include  modifying civil commitment and "aid and assist" laws to broaden the criteria
for detaining and treating individuals with severe mental illness. It facilitates involuntary care
by redefining "danger to self or others" and sets new statutory timelines for holding individuals
in the state hospital.  The law was designed to expedite care for individuals in crisis while
attempting to stabilize the state’s behavioral health system.
Senate Bill 1561 –              Replacing Dwellings Damaged by Natural Causes –
Effective January 1, 2027
Senate Bill 1561, passed in 2026, applies to applications to replace a dwelling that has been
damaged or destroyed by a natural or involuntary event.                                                 The City already has compliant
provisions to address rebuilding non-                                          conforming residential development and densities.
However, for non-                             conforming residential uses, those non-conforming rights may be lost when
the repair cost of the structure is more than 75 percent of its assessed value      . This project will
modify the non-conforming regulations to conform to the bill.

Section II: Proposals

The State Land Use Compliance P  roject includes 13 proposals, which are divided into three
broad categories:
Category 1: Middle Housing Development Standards              largely addresses HB 2138,                                                                                                            including
updating the allowed configurations for middle                                             housing, adding new required bonuses for
middle housing , and chang           ing some development standards that apply to middle housing land
divisions (MHLD).
Category 2: Land Use Review Procedures          pertains to procedural changes  required by HB              2138,
SB 974, and                                                                                  HB 4037, such as                            the timing of Middle                                                                           Housing Land                       Division applications,
consolidating land division reviews, and             revising notification, public hearing and appeal
regulations.
Category 3: Allowed Uses           responds to HB              2005                            and SB 1561                            by expanding                                                        where residential
treatment facilities are allowed and making it easier to replace a dwelling that has been
damaged or destroyed by             natural causes.
The focus of this project is making changes that are necessary                 to comply with state  statutes.
While there may be other development standards or processes that could                                                also be improved to
facilitate implementation, some of t          hese changes necessitate more analysis,               community
engagement and deliberation before recommending a course of action                                                 . Given the impending
deadline for compliance , a more comprehensive review is not possible . These additional,                                        non-
mandated changes will need to be part of future    code change proposals.
The following provides additional information regarding the proposals within each of the                three
categories.

Section III: Public Outreach

Due to the short amount                                 of time allotted for cities to conform to the state requirements, and
because of the narrow focus of this project scope, public outreach is more limited. Typical
legislative projects often include a public “discussion draft” whe n the public can comment and
make suggestions for improvements to proposed code changes or                  provide feedback on the
merits of certain policy choices. Since the State Land Use Compliance Package is focused on
bringing the city’s zoning code into conformance with state legislation, there is                   little discretion
for policy choices.
Consequently, this project did not include a Discussion Draft               . Outreach has included
information materials on the project website beginning  in May 2026, updates provided to the
BPS e-news list serve, and public notice and  a public hearing             before the Planning Commission’s
deliberation and recommendations.
Additional opportunities for public input will be available at the City Council Committee hearing
and finally, at City Council prior to the adoption of the proposed code                                            amendments.

33.110.100.B Limited Uses
House Bill 2005, passed in the 2025 Oregon Legislative session, supersedes local zoning for
siting “residential treatment homes” and “residential treatment facilities. The bill  states that
jurisdictions must allow residential treatment homes and residential treatment facilities on
lands that are zoned for residential, commercial, and employment uses.

The zoning code considers residential treatment homes (defined as 5 or fewer individuals) as a
household living use, but residential treatment facilities (defined as 6 or more individuals) are
classified as a group living use. Currently, the single-dwelling zones limit the size that a group
living use is allowed by-right to 3,500 square feet of floor area. Larger group living uses are
subject to a Conditional Use review.

This amendment revises the use limitations in the single dwelling zones to allow residential
treatment facilities by-right in conformance with the provisions of HB 2005. The use would
still be required to meet the development standards of the zone like other allowed uses.

Note that the multi -dwelling and commercial zones allow household living and group living uses
by-right so no additional changes are required for those base zones.

Multi-dwelling development
While the prior middle housing bill [HB2001, 2019 session] allowed cities to define middle
housing “duplexes, triplexes, and fourplexes”” as either attached or detached units,
HB 2138 [Section 1, (1)], now requires that cities allow for “attached or detached dwellings
in any configuration”.

For the purposes of the city’s zoning code, “duplexes, triplexes, and fourplexes” will
continue to refer to units that are in a single building (i.e. attached configurations). Other
configurations of units in multiple buildings on a single lot are defined as “multi-dwelling
development”. Consequently, multi-dwelling development will now be allowed in the R20-
R2.5 zones.

Also, HB 2138 includes provisions to add up to two bonus units for fourplexes. For the
purposes of the city’s zoning code, “fourplexes” will continue to be limited to four units,
but additional units will be allowed within a multi-dwelling structure (5 or more units in a
single building) or multi-dwelling development (multiple units in two or more buildings) when
the requirements for bonus units have been met.

Cottage clusters
House Bill 2138 amended ORS 197A.420 pertaining to where middle housing must be
allowed [Section 1, (2)]. Where previously, middle housing was required “in areas” (later
defined through administrative rulemaking guidelines), middle housing including cottage
clusters is now required “on each lot or parcel zoned for residential use”.

This means that the previous limitations for cottage clusters that applied in the R20 zone
must be removed. Applicants proposing middle housing including cottage clusters must still
show how sufficient infrastructure will be provided to serve the development. Middle
housing other than duplexes is also limited in certain goal-protected areas where natural
hazards or resources are present (see Chapter 33.418).

Middle housing continues to be restricted in the RF zone as this is intended to generally
be an agricultural zone, and not subject to ORS 197A.420 or HB 2138.

33.110.226 Bonus Options
HB 2138 [Section 3] requires bonus units be offered when accessible units or affordable
units are proposed.

Floor area bonuses previously existed in the City’s single dwelling zones for providing one
affordable unit (at 60% median family income) or preserving an existing house while adding
additional dwelling units. Furthermore, in the residential infill options section (33.110.265)

when half of the units were provided as affordable units (at 60% median family income).

This new section restructures and consolidates these bonuses and adds new bonuses for
accessible units.

For cottage clusters, FAR and building coverage limits do not apply. So these incentives
are not applicable. Provisions to earn additional dwelling units are included in the cottage
cluster standards and match the requirements shown in Subsections. D. and F.

For middle housing sites                   (e.g. duplex, triplex, fourplex) bonus FAR and coverage applies to
the development on the site, not just the unit or units that qualified for the bonus.

For attached houses which are dwelling units on separate lots with a common shared wall,
building coverage is calculated separately for each lot but may distributed among the units
that share a common wall.

The FAR for attached houses is determined by the total number of units on the site, and
any additional floor area from a bonus is calculated based on the size of the lot with the
unit that qualified for the bonus. The total floor area may be distributed among all
attached house lots on the site. See 33.110.265.C for more information.

33.110.226.C. Affordable dwelling unit bonus.
This slightly modifies the previously available bonus for projects that included at least one
dwelling unit that was affordable to households earning up to 60% of the median family
income. To differentiate this bonus from the deeper affordability bonus (now also 1 unit
@60%MFI), this has been changed to 100% MFI. Projects meeting this requirement earn
additional FAR only.

33.110.226.D. Deeper housing           affordability bonus
This bonus includes a slight revision to the previous deeper affordability bonus that
allowed additional dwelling units, height/coverage, and FAR for units that were affordable
to households earning up to 60% of the median family income. The previous bonus required
half of the units to be affordable to qualify, whereas the revised bonus will require just
one unit to qualify. Each deeply affordable unit may earn additional FAR, up to the total
bonus maximum. The revised FAR bonus structure is also tailored to the FARs for each of
the base zones and total units proposed instead of the existing single maximum FAR of 1.2
that applied.

33.110.226.E. Adaptable dwelling unit bonus
To complement the Accessible unit bonus, this bonus offers bonus building coverage and
FAR for units that meet the Type B dwelling unit standards as found in the ICC A.117 code
and referenced in the Oregon Structural Specialty Code. Type B units include some
accessibility features and are designed to be more adaptable by future residents.
Additional clarification is included that the bonus is not intended to require all floors of
the dwelling unit to be adaptable (which would necessitate a lift or elevator). For the
purposes of this bonus, if there are multiple floors, only one floor of the dwelling unit that
is located on the accessible route must include a kitchen, bathroom with bath or shower,
and a bedroom.

33.110.226.F. Accessible dwelling unit bonus
For proposals that provide Accessible Type A units, in addition to the increased building
coverage, greater FAR increases are allowed (along with additional dwelling units in
33.110.265). Type A units provide a higher degree of accessibility features and more
clearance space to accommodate mobility devices. Like the adaptable units, accessible
units are allowed to have multiple floors as long as the accessible floor meets the Type A
requirements and includes a kitchen, bathroom with bath or shower, and a bedroom.

33.110.265.D. Duplexes.
With the changes required by HB 2138 which specify that middle housing be allowed in any
configuration of attached or detached units, the provisions that established the conditions
when detached units are allowed (associated with an existing house, lower building height)
are removed.

33.110.265.E. Triplexes and fourplexes
A distinguishing characteristic for middle housing with three or more units is the
requirement that the site be located on a maintained street. This requirement ensures
that sufficient access is available to serve the proposed development and implements OAR
660-046-0020. Sites that do not have frontage on at least one maintained street will not
be eligible for three or more units because areas with unimproved streets create
impediments to access and mobility, reducing walkability and rollability. While new
development and conversions to add units could trigger the need for a street improvement
or payment of a fee-in lieu (LTIC), the incremental improvements could be mid-block and
not connect to the street network or the LTIC improvement may occur elsewhere.

33.110.265.E.1. Triplexes and fourplexes are defined as three and four units in a
building respectively. Noting the allowed density is redundant, and is deleted.

33.110.265.E.2. HB 2138 requires that middle housing be allowed on each lot or parcel
where a detached single unit dwelling is allowed. Therefore, lot size standards that differ
for middle housing are not permissible and are deleted.

33.110.265.E.3. Previously, middle housing with 3 or more units required that a certain
percentage of the units meet “visitability” standards. However, with the introduction of
required HB 2138 accessibility bonuses, which are based on standards embedded in the
state building code, it is impractical to apply two sets of accessibility standards, one
implemented through building code standards, with the other implemented through zoning
code standards. Therefore, the visitability requirement is being deleted.

33.110.165.H Cottage Cluster
HB 2138 requires middle housing, including cottage clusters, to be allowed on each lot or
parcel where a detached single unit dwelling is allowed. Consequently, they are expanded to
include the R20 zones. The RF zone is generally an agricultural zone and not subject to
HB 2138. Like other types of middle housing with three or more units, frontage on a
maintained street remains a requirement.

33.110.165.H.1 Maximum Area. The requirement that clusters be allowed wherever a
detached dwelling is allowed also means that separate lot size or dimension requirements
that differ from detached single unit dwellings are not allowed.

33.110.165.H.2. Minimum Density. HB 2138 requires duplexes, triplexes and fourplexes
be allowed in “any configuration.” (allowed in this code as “multi-dwelling development”).
Consequently, there is little distinguishing a 3 or 4 unit cottage cluster from a 3 or 4 unit
multi-dwelling development, apart from specific cottage cluster requirements that apply to
one type of development versus FAR and building coverage (and other general standards)
that applied to the other. To maintain clear distinctions, cottage clusters are now defined
as containing 5 or more units. While there remains some overlap between non-cottage
cluster development that has earned 1 or 2 bonus units, OAR 660-046-0205 only permits
cities to establish a minimum of 3, 4 or 5 units within a cottage cluster. Nevertheless,
outside the bonus unit scenario, this 5-unit minimum for cottage clusters creates a
brighter distinguishing line between development types than what existed with 3 units.

Table 110-8 is changed to add minimum density for the R20 zone, as this zone now allows
cottage clusters. However, it should be noted that with the 5-unit minimum, and the
maximum site size of 1 acre, minimum density would automatically be met in the R20, R10
and R7 zones.

33.110.165.H.8 Building coverage.
a. With the changes that allow up to four dwelling units to be included in a single
structure, the previous coverage limit that applied to individual detached dwelling units
(900 sq ft) no longer works since units can be stacked or arranged side by side in the
structure. Instead, maximum building coverage will apply to each structure that contains
dwelling units and will be determined by the number of dwelling units in that structure.
This coverage limit applies to                                      any attached accessory structures such as garages.

b. Detached and connected accessory structures are intended to be smaller and incidental
to the primary structures. This standard previously referred to the building coverage
footprint to be no larger than the smallest (detached individual) primary dwelling unit.
However, now since up to 4 dwelling units may be located in a single building, those primary
buildings may have up to 3,600 square feet of coverage each. Consequently, a smaller
maximum 400 sq ft coverage limit is applied to accessory structures, unless there is a
smaller primary structure on the site. The 400 sq ft limit aligns with the maximum floor
area limit of 400 sq ft for accessory structures in H.5.

33.110.165.H.9 Common outdoor area.
With the increase of units required to make a cottage cluster from 3 to 5, the minimum
size for the outdoor area was proportionately increased. (3x150 = 450, 5x150 = 750).
When units are closer than 10 feet from each other this creates opportunities for
additional common open area. Consequently, there are no proposed changes to the current
distinction of required common area (150 sq ft vs 200 sq ft) when units are attached (i.e.
closer than 10 feet from each other).

33.120.270.F.3. Dwelling unit configuration. A new paragraph is added to respond to
definitional changes to cottage clusters. HB 2138 Section 1(1) revised the definition of
cottage clusters to mean “a grouping of dwelling units that are detached or attached in
subgroupings of up to four units in any configuration” where previously all units had to be
detached from each other. Note that the four unit limit aligns with the distinction for a
multi-dwelling structure (defined as 5 or more units in a building). This language mirrors
what is specified for cottage clusters in the single dwelling zones.

33.120.270.F.6 Separation. Previously each dwelling unit had to be separated from the
other dwelling units. This change now applies the separation requirement between buildings
(which may contain multiple units).

33.120.270.F.7 Building coverage.
a. With the changes that allow up to four dwelling units to be included in a single
structure, the previous coverage limit that applied to individual detached dwelling units
(900 sq ft) no longer works since units can be stacked or arranged side by side in the
structure. Instead, maximum building coverage will apply to each structure that contains
dwelling units and will be determined by the number of dwelling units in that structure.
This coverage limit applies to any attached accessory structures such as garages.

b. Detached and connected accessory structures are intended to be smaller and incidental
to the primary structures. This standard previously referred to the building coverage
footprint to be no larger than the smallest (detached individual) primary dwelling unit.
However, now since up to 4 dwelling units may be located in a single building, those primary
buildings may have up to 3,600 square feet of coverage each. Consequently, a smaller
maximum 400 sq ft coverage limit is applied to accessory structures, unless there is a
smaller primary structure on the site. The 400 sq ft limit aligns with the maximum floor
area limit of 400 sq ft for accessory structures in F.4.

33.140.100.B Limited Uses
Currently, the EG1 & EG2 Employment zones prohibit household living and group living uses
unless they are the result of a conversion of a temporary lodging facility, which was a
requirement of House Bill 3261 (2021).

House Bill 2005, passed in the 2025 Oregon Legislative session, states that jurisdictions must
allow residential treatment homes and residential treatment facilities on lands that are zoned
for residential, commercial, and employment uses.

The zoning code considers residential treatment homes (defined as 5 or fewer individuals) as a
household living use, but residential treatment facilities (defined as 6 or more individuals) are
classified as a group living use. As mentioned previously, these are prohibited in most cases in
the EG zones. However, the EG zones are intended to allow a range of employment uses. Per HB
2005, that means that these zones should also allow residential treatment homes and facilities
without requiring a zone change, plan amendment or conditional use review. This amendment
revises the prohibition in our EG zones to allow residential treatment homes and facilities by
right to conform with the provisions of HB 2005.

Note, a similar provision is not incorporated into the industrial zones. HB 2005 does not
require jurisdictions to allow this use in industrial zones that are specifically designated for
heavy industrial uses. All of the I-Industrial zones allow a wide range of industrial uses
including heavy industrial uses, and the I zones are generally within the Industrial Sanctuary
comprehensive plan designation. So, they would not be suitable lands for residential treatment
or uses.

33.253.030 Applying Title 33 to Development on a Middle Housing Land Division Site
A middle housing land division is used to separate a middle housing site into separate “fee-
simple” lots. However, unlike a regular land division, for the purposes of applying
development standards, the development on each of the middle housing land division lots is
viewed in combination with the remaining lots that were part of the land division, as though
the middle housing land division property lines do not exist.

Changes introduced by HB 2138 enabled consolidated regular land divisions (to create
“parent” lots) with concurrent middle housing land divisions of each of the parent lots into
smaller lots for middle housing. For example, a four lot subdivision proposed with each lot
divided for fourplex middle housing lots. The amendment to this section makes the
distinction between the two types of land divisions more clear.

33.253.040 Number of Dwelling Units
HB 2138 Section 14 amended ORS 92.031                (2)(d) to include an exception to the
requirement that MHLD lots only have a single dwelling unit. This exception allows an
existing house with an existing accessory dwelling unit, or an existing duplex to be located
on a lot, essentially allowing two units on the lot.

It should be noted that when additional dwelling units are added to a site with a house or
duplex, the residential structure type for the site changes. For example, adding two
detached units on a site with an existing duplex would make that site “multi-dwelling
development”. However, for the purposes of this exception, the existing units must have
originally been permitted as either a house/ADU or duplex.

HB 2138 Section 14 also allows a city to “prohibit or add approval criteria to the allowance
of a new accessory dwelling unit on a lot or parcel resulting from a middle housing land
division.” Aside from incorporating any existing ADUs, this change would not allow new
ADUs on MHLD lots. Such an approach would add too many additional variables and levels
of code complexity. Additionally, since most MHLD sites have some shared utility services,
additional ADUs that were not originally planned for could negatively impact the private
services of other units on the MHLD site, and since HB 2138 prohibits restrictive CC&Rs,
the developer nor the subsequent owners could legally limit that impact from occurring.

33.258.050 Non-conforming Uses
SB 1561 Section 2 amends ORS 197 and requires a local government to allow replacement
of dwelling units destroyed by a natural or involuntary event                 to be replaced within the

replaced in zones that allow residential uses. However, where residential uses are

fire or other causes beyond the control of the owner is only allowed when the repair cost
does not exceed 75% of assessed value. This amendment brings the nonconforming use
regulations related to replacement of dwelling units in line with the requirements of
SB 1561.

SB 1561 sets time limits on replacement related to application for replacement (3 years)
and the beginning                    of construction (4 years). For ease of implementation of the zoning code,
this amendment sets a 5 year time limit                 for replacement.

33.508.130 Additional Prohibited Uses
This section lists additional uses that are prohibited in the Cascade Station/Portland
International Center plan district. At the time these regulations were adopted, the EG zones
allowed household and group living uses through a conditional use review. The intent at the time

limitations in the plan district are duplicative. Removing this specific plan district regulation
means that the limitations applicable to the base EG zones for household and group living apply.
This makes the plan district consistent with the requirements of HB 2005 that require zones
for employment uses to allow residential treatment homes and facilities (see 33.140.100).

ORS92.031 defines a “middle housing land division” to mean a partition or subdivision of a

ORS197A.420(7) permits cities to allow middle housing in areas that are not otherwise

This means that middle housing is not required in the Commercial/Mixed Use zones but
that cities may additionally allow it in these zones. Portland allows duplexes, triplexes, and

under-utilized, extremely parcelized lands that make redevelopment into higher intensity
mixed use development more difficult.

with up to 6 dwelling units and multi dwelling structures with up to 6 dwelling units are
included in the types of housing that can be divided through a middle housing land division.

33.644.030 Timing of Development with a Middle Housing Land Division
HB 2138 Section 14(4)(e) requires a city or county to allow submission of a tentative plan
for a middle housing land division before, at the same time or after submission of building
permits for the middle housing. This new section makes that allowance clear with one
exception: When a concurrent land division and middle housing land division will be
processed, the regular land division must be finalized before there are additional lots
available to construct the middle housing. In this case, the regular land division and the
middle housing land division can be processed concurrently, but the building permits for
the middle housing cannot be processed at the same time. The building permits cannot be
processed until after the regular land division has been finalized.

33.660.110 Review Procedures
House Bill 4037, passed by the Oregon legislature in 2026, specifies limits on, and
requirements for, notification pertaining to decisions about residential development made
through clear and objective standards. Land divisions are considered “limited land use
decisions” and jurisdictions are required by ORS 197.195 to send notice of the decision.

“(3)(b) For limited land use decisions, the local government shall provide written notice to
owners of property within 100 feet of the entire contiguous site for which the application
is made…”

HB 4037 did not remove this ORS 197.195 requirements. Instead, it limits the extent of
notice that is allowed, prohibits local governments from holding a hearing, and allows the
applicant, and only the applicant, to                                    appeal the local governments decision to LUBA.

The most similar type of review procedure in this case is the Type I or Type Ix review.
Based on the need for internal coordinated review for land divisions (notably for service
and access requirements), and for consistency with the current Type Ix review that
applies to land divisions, the longer Type Ix review (45 versus 21 day review period) is
proposed.

33.910.030 Definitions
Residential Facility
Residential Home
House Bill 2005, which was passed in the 2025 legislative session made changes within the
land use statutes, ORS 197 and 197A, specific to residential homes and residential
facilities. New and amended rules applicable to residential treatment homes and
residential treatment facilities were added to these sections and residential treatment
facility rules were separated from other types of residential facilities. Consequently,
jurisdictions must treat residential treatment homes and residential treatment facilities
differently than other state defined residential homes and facilities. (see summary below)

Cottage Cluster
With changes to triplexes and fourplexes that require cities allow “any configuration” of
attached or detached units, there is considerable overlap between these housing types and
3 and 4 unit cottage clusters. To minimize the potential for confusion, Cottage Clusters
are being redefined to require at least five dwelling units. The maximum unit limit is
increased from 16 to 18 units to account for potential bonus units, also required by
HB 2138, that are described in the single dwelling base zone.

Cottage clusters are similar to multi-dwelling development, but unlike multi-dwelling
developments, cottage clusters include specific additional requirements (see comparison in
the commentary for housing types in the multi-dwelling zones on page 68.) Additionally,
while cottage clusters are eligible for a middle housing land division, multi-dwelling
development (above 6 units) is not.

Duplex
A middle housing land division does not change the residential structure type. For example,
a duplex (2 units on a regular lot) that is divided through a middle housing land division (1
unit on each MHLD lot) does not become “attached houses” each on their own lot. They
remain and are regulated as a duplex, with FAR, building coverage, setbacks and other
standards based on the combination of both MHLD lots together. The State’s statutory
definition of middle housing includes “townhouses” which is a useful reference for “plex”
units that have been divided through an MHLD and remain a plex, and is also useful to
differentiate them from “attached houses”. Since “townhouses” and divided “plexes” are
essentially the same thing, there is no need to create an additional residential structure
type definition for “townhouse”.
